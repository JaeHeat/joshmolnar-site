Gambler's Fallacy in Trading: You Are Not Due a Win

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 6 min read

You take five losing trades in a row. Something in your head says the next one has to work. You have paid your dues. A win is owed. That feeling has a name. It is the gambler’s fallacy in trading, and it has quietly emptied more accounts than any bad chart pattern I know of. I have felt it myself, and almost everyone I mentor has felt it too. So here is what it is, why your brain falls for it, and the simple rules I use to keep it away from my money.

What is the gambler’s fallacy in trading?

The gambler’s fallacy is the belief that after a run of one result, the opposite result is due. Flip a coin and get heads five times. Most people feel that tails is now more likely. It is not. The coin has no memory. The next flip is still a fifty-fifty shot, the same as the first one.

In trading it sounds like this.

  • I have lost four in a row, so this one has to win.
  • Price has gone up six days straight, so it has to drop tomorrow.
  • My setup has not worked all week, so it is about to pay me back.

Every one of those is the same mistake. You are treating the market like it keeps score and owes you something. It does not know you exist.

The night black came up 26 times

The most famous example happened at the Monte Carlo Casino in 1913. On one roulette table the ball landed on black 26 times in a row. As the run got longer, more and more players piled their money onto red. Their logic felt airtight. Black could not possibly come up again.

It did. Again and again. The wheel was not broken and it was not being kind or cruel. Each spin was a fresh spin. The players who kept raising their bets on red lost more with every turn, because they were betting on a debt the wheel never owed them.

Why your brain falls for it

Your brain loves balance. It expects luck to even out fast, right now, in the next few tries. Over thousands of flips a fair coin does land close to half heads and half tails. But it gets there by piling up many new flips, not by fixing the old ones. Nothing is ever paid back.

Here is a picture that helps. Flip a coin 100 times and you will very likely see five tails in a row somewhere in the list. That is not a broken coin. That is what normal luck looks like. Streaks are part of random results, not a sign that something is about to turn.

The same is true for a real strategy. Even a good one that wins about half the time will hand you ugly runs of losses. I wrote about living through those in how to handle a losing streak.

How the gambler’s fallacy costs traders money

The belief alone does no harm. The damage comes from what people do next.

  • Betting bigger after losses. If the next trade is a sure thing, why not double the size? This is how a normal bad week turns into a blown account.
  • Taking weaker trades. You stop waiting for your real setup because any trade feels like the one that is due.
  • Fighting a strong move. You sell because price has gone up too many days in a row, with no other reason. Strong moves can run much longer than feels fair.
  • Adding to a loser. It has dropped so far that it must bounce. So you buy more of the thing that is already hurting you.

Notice the first one. Doubling up after losses is the same engine that drives revenge trading. One is powered by anger and the other by bad math, but the account ends up in the same place.

One honest difference between markets and coins

A market is not a coin. Conditions change, and sometimes a run of losses is telling you something real. Maybe the market went quiet and your setup needs movement. That is worth checking, calmly, after the session.

But look at which way that lesson points. If a losing run means anything, it means slow down and look. It never means the next trade is more likely to win. Either the streak is plain bad luck, and the next trade has the same odds as always. Or something has changed, and the odds may be worse. Neither one is a reason to bet bigger.

What I do instead

I do not try to out-think this feeling in the moment. I build rules that make it harmless.

  • Same risk on every trade. My size does not go up after losses. It is set before the day starts. Fixed risk is one of the pieces I cover on my page about trading for a living.
  • The setup decides, not the streak. I ask one question. Would I take this trade if my last five were winners? If the answer is no, I skip it.
  • A daily stopping point. After a set number of losses I am done for the day. No trade gets to be the one that fixes everything.
  • Judge in big batches. I look at my results over 50 or 100 trades, never over the last five. Five trades tell you almost nothing.

None of this makes losing fun. It just stops a bad streak from changing how I trade. Most traders lose money, and a big reason is that a normal run of bad luck pushes them into bigger and sloppier bets. The cure is boring. Treat every trade as a fresh spin, risk the same small amount, and let a large pile of trades tell you the truth.

Common questions

What is the gambler's fallacy in trading?

It is the belief that after a run of losses a win is due, or that a price that has risen many days in a row must fall next. Past results do not make the opposite result more likely.

Is a winning trade more likely after a losing streak?

No. A losing streak does not raise the odds of the next trade. Either it was normal bad luck and the odds are the same, or conditions changed and the odds may be worse.

What is an example of the gambler's fallacy?

At the Monte Carlo Casino in 1913 a roulette ball landed on black 26 times in a row. Players kept betting more on red because they felt it was due, and they kept losing.

How do I avoid the gambler's fallacy when trading?

Risk the same small amount on every trade, take only your planned setup, stop for the day after a set number of losses, and judge your results over 50 to 100 trades instead of the last few.

Should I increase my position size after a few losses?

No. Raising your size because a win feels due is how a normal bad week becomes a blown account. Keep your risk the same on every trade.
