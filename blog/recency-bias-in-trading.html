Recency Bias in Trading: Why Your Last Trade Lies

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 6 min read

Three losses in a row and my strategy feels broken. Three wins in a row and I feel like I finally figured the market out. Neither feeling is true. Both come from the same mental trap, and it has a name. Recency bias in trading is the habit of treating whatever just happened as if it were the whole story. It is one of the quietest ways traders talk themselves out of a good plan, so here is how it works and what I do about it.

What is recency bias in trading?

Your brain gives extra weight to fresh memories. What happened this morning feels bigger and more real than what happened three months ago. That is useful if you just touched a hot stove. It is a problem in a game where the last few results tell you almost nothing.

Picture a coin. Flip it five times and you might get four heads. Does that mean the coin now lands on heads most of the time? Of course not. Five flips is too small a sample to tell you anything. You would need hundreds of flips to see what the coin really does.

Your trades work the same way. A handful of recent trades is a tiny sample. But because those trades are fresh, and because they came with real money and real feelings, they shout louder than the hundreds of older trades sitting quietly in your records.

How recency bias shows up at the screen

I have done every one of these, and I see them constantly in the people I mentor.

  • Quitting a plan after a few losses. A method that worked across a hundred trades gets thrown away because of the last four. Then the next method gets the same treatment a month later.
  • Betting bigger after a few wins. A hot week feels like proof of skill, so the size goes up right before the normal run of losses arrives.
  • Skipping the next trade. The last trade lost, so the next good chance looks scary and you watch it work without you.
  • Believing today is forever. The market fell hard this week, so it feels like it can only fall. It ran up this week, so it feels like it can only go up.

It is a cousin of the gambler’s fallacy, but it points the other way. The gambler’s fallacy says a change is due. Recency bias says the recent run will keep going. Both make the same mistake. They read meaning into a few results that do not carry any.

Why your last few trades are bad evidence

Here is the piece that changed how I think. Losing runs are normal, even for a method that works. Imagine a plan that wins half the time. Over a hundred trades, a run of five losses in a row is more likely than not to show up at least once. That is not the plan breaking. That is just what chance looks like.

So when five losses arrive, the feeling says something is wrong. The math says this was always on the schedule. If you change your plan every time chance does what chance does, you will never stay with anything long enough to learn whether it works.

The same is true in reverse. A great week does not mean you got better. It might mean you did. It might mean the coin landed your way a few times. One week cannot tell you which.

How I keep recency bias out of my decisions

I do not try to stop the feeling. I cannot. What I can do is make sure the feeling is not the one making the call.

  • I judge in big batches. I look at my results in blocks of at least 30 to 50 trades, never one day or one week. A single trade is one coin flip. A block of trades is real information.
  • I write everything down. A trading journal is the cure for a loud memory. When I feel like nothing is working, I open the record and look at the full picture. It is almost always calmer than my mood.
  • I decide in advance what counts as broken. Before I trade a method, I write down the longest losing run I should expect from it. If the current run is inside that number, I keep going. If it is far outside, then I stop and study it.
  • I keep my risk the same. My size does not go up after wins or down after losses. Same small slice every trade. That one rule takes away most of the damage this bias can do.
  • I never change rules in the middle of the day. Changes get made on the weekend, with the records open and the market closed.

The bottom line

Recency bias in trading never goes away. I still feel it after every loss and every win. The goal is not to feel nothing. The goal is to let a big pile of trades make the decisions, and to treat the last few as what they are, which is noise. Most traders lose money, and a big reason is that they keep steering by the last thing that happened. Staying with a tested plan through a normal rough patch is not exciting, but it is a large part of what it takes to trade for a living. None of this makes a win certain. It only makes sure your decisions come from evidence and not from your mood.

Common questions

What is recency bias in trading?

It is the habit of giving your most recent trades far more weight than your older ones. A few fresh wins or losses start to feel like the whole truth, even though they are too small a sample to prove anything.

What is an example of recency bias in trading?

Dropping a plan that worked over a hundred trades because the last four lost. Another common one is trading bigger after a hot week because the wins feel like proof of skill.

How do you overcome recency bias in trading?

Judge your results in blocks of 30 to 50 trades, keep a written journal, and keep your risk the same on every trade. Decide ahead of time how long a losing run is normal for your method so a rough patch does not push you to change the rules.

Is recency bias the same as the gambler's fallacy?

No. The gambler's fallacy says a change is due after a streak, while recency bias says the streak will keep going. Both read meaning into a handful of results that do not carry any.
