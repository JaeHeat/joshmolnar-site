How to Trade Through a Drawdown

By Josh Molnar · October 2026 · 6 min read

Every trader hits a drawdown. It does not matter how good your strategy is, how long you have been doing this, or how confident you felt last week. At some point your account will be red, your recent trades will mostly be losses, and you will feel the pull to do something drastic. What you do inside that drawdown is the difference between a bad month and a blown account. So let me walk you through exactly how I trade through a drawdown.

What a drawdown actually is

A drawdown is the distance between your account high and where it sits right now. If your account peaked at 50,000 dollars and today it is at 46,000, you are in an 8 percent drawdown. That is it. It is not a crisis, it is not proof your strategy is broken, and it is not a reason to change everything. It is the normal cost of doing business as a trader. The problem is never the drawdown itself. The problem is what traders do inside one.

Step one: cut your size

The single most important move inside a drawdown is to trade smaller. Not bigger. Smaller. Most traders do the opposite. They feel behind, they want to win it back fast, so they size up. That is the fastest way to turn a 5 percent dip into a 20 percent hole you cannot climb out of.

My rule is simple. If I am down more than 3 to 4 percent from my equity high, I cut my risk per trade in half. If the drawdown gets worse, I cut again. The goal is not to make money right now. The goal is to stop the bleeding so the account is still alive when the edge comes back. I explain the math behind sizing in how much to risk per trade, and it matters twice as much during a drawdown.

Step two: tighten your filter

When things are going well, you might take your best setups and a few second-tier trades. During a drawdown, you only take the best. Nothing else. If a setup does not check every single box on your trading plan, you skip it. Period.

This does two things. It keeps you from overtrading, which is the number one way drawdowns spiral. And it forces you back to the core of your edge, the setups that actually work over time, instead of chasing random ideas because you feel desperate.

Step three: stop checking your P&L

This sounds small but it is not. When you are in a drawdown and you check your account balance every ten minutes, every red tick feels personal. It makes you tighten stops too early, skip valid trades out of fear, or revenge trade to get back to some round number.

During a drawdown, I check my account once a day, after the session is done. I focus on the process. Did I follow my rules? Did I take only A-grade setups? Did I size correctly? If the answers are yes, the results will eventually follow. If I start making decisions based on the P&L number instead of the plan, the drawdown always gets worse.

Step four: review your journal, not your emotions

A drawdown feels like your strategy is broken. Sometimes it is. Most of the time it is not. The only way to tell the difference is to look at the data, not the feeling. Open your trading journal and ask one question. Am I following my rules and still losing, or am I breaking rules?

If you are following your rules and losing, that is just the normal ups and downs of any real edge. No strategy wins every week. You keep going. If you are breaking rules, then the drawdown is not a strategy problem. It is a discipline problem. Fix the behavior first. The numbers will follow. I wrote a full breakdown on how to keep a trading journal that makes this kind of review easy.

When to stop trading entirely

There is a line where cutting size is not enough and you need to step away. For me, that line is a daily loss limit. If I hit my max loss for the day, I am done. No exceptions. If you trade prop firm accounts, this is not optional. Breach the drawdown rule and you lose the account, full stop. But even on personal capital, a hard daily stop protects you from the worst version of yourself.

If the drawdown continues across multiple days and you feel emotional about every trade, take a day or two off the screens entirely. The market will be there when you come back. Your account might not be if you keep forcing trades from a bad headspace.

What not to do

I have made all of these mistakes, so I can list them clearly.

  • Do not size up to win it back. That is revenge trading with extra steps.
  • Do not switch strategies. A drawdown is the worst time to test something new. Stick with what you know.
  • Do not set a recovery deadline. Telling yourself you need to be back to even by Friday creates pressure that leads to bad trades.
  • Do not compare yourself to other traders. Someone on social media is always green when you are red. It means nothing about your edge.

The bottom line

A drawdown is not the enemy. Your reaction to it is. Cut your size, tighten your filter, focus on process over P&L, and use your journal to separate real problems from normal variance. The traders who survive drawdowns are not the ones who never have them. They are the ones who refuse to make a bad stretch worse. That patience, that discipline, is what keeps you in the game long enough to come out the other side.

Common questions

How long do trading drawdowns usually last?

It depends on the strategy and market conditions. Some drawdowns last a few days, others a few weeks. The key is not to force a timeline on recovery. Focus on following your rules and the equity curve will take care of itself.

Should I stop trading during a drawdown?

Not automatically. Cut your size and tighten your setup filter first. But if you hit your daily loss limit or feel too emotional to follow your plan, stepping away for a day or two is the right move.

How much drawdown is normal for a day trader?

A drawdown of 5 to 10 percent is common even for experienced traders. If your drawdown is consistently larger than that, it usually points to oversizing or a lack of a daily loss limit.

Is a drawdown a sign my strategy is broken?

Not necessarily. Every strategy goes through losing stretches. Check your trading journal. If you are following your rules and still losing, it is likely normal variance. If you are breaking rules, fix the behavior first.
