Does Technical Analysis Work? An Honest Answer

By Josh Molnar · October 2026 · 6 min read

I am a data scientist who trades full time. That means I have spent years running the numbers on trading ideas before committing real money to them. And the question I get asked more than almost any other is a simple one. Does technical analysis actually work?

I am going to give you the honest answer. It is a little more complicated than a yes or a no, because most of the discussion around this topic is either too dismissive or too credulous.

What technical analysis is actually claiming

Technical analysis is the idea that you can find useful information in past price data. A chart, a moving average, a support level, a candlestick pattern. All of these tools are claims that certain price behaviors tend to repeat.

That claim is not crazy. Markets are made of people, and people do repeat certain behaviors. Panic selling looks the same in 2008 and 2024. That is the seed of truth that keeps technical analysis alive.

The honest problem with most technical analysis

Here is where I have to be blunt. Most traders use technical analysis as a storytelling tool, not a tested one. They look at a chart, see a pattern, and build a story around it. The pattern feels convincing because they already chose it after the move happened.

Think of it like reading a finished book and calling yourself a prophet. Of course the chart looks obvious in hindsight. The real question is whether the pattern, applied consistently before the move, would have made money over hundreds of trades. That is a much harder test to pass.

I have run those tests. Many popular patterns do not pass. Classic flag patterns, head-and-shoulders setups, standard candlestick signals. Some show a tiny consistent edge. Most show something close to random. The ones that do work tend to work only in specific conditions that most people never bother to define in advance.

What actually has evidence behind it

Some tools in technical analysis hold up when you test them honestly:

  • Trend following has a real track record. Buying assets that are going up and selling those going down has decades of evidence across many markets. It does not work every year, but the long-run edge is real.
  • Support and resistance zones have some validity. Not because charts are magic, but because enough traders watch the same levels and react at them. That reaction becomes real through repetition.
  • Volume confirms moves. A breakout on heavy trading activity tends to hold better than one on thin activity. This is one of the more consistent findings I have seen across markets.
  • Session timing matters. Markets behave differently at the open, midday, and close. Knowing when your setups tend to work is more useful than most chart patterns.

Notice what is not on that list. Complex multi-indicator systems. Fibonacci ratios as price magnets. “Confluences” of five signals all pointing the same way. The more complicated a setup is, the easier it is to find it on any chart after the fact, and the harder it is to test honestly.

Why people keep using technical analysis even when it does not work

Because it feels like it works. And that feeling is the biggest trap in trading.

Our brains are wired to find patterns. Show a person 50 random coin flips and they will find a pattern in them. Show them a price chart and they will find a setup. The problem is not the tools. The problem is that we remember our winning trades and forget our losing ones. We look at a chart after a big move and count all the times our indicator gave the right signal, not the times it failed.

The only way out of this trap is to keep a detailed trading journal and track every single signal your setup gives, not just the ones you acted on. I write about how to keep a trading journal in detail elsewhere. It is the closest thing I know to an honest mirror for your trading.

The real answer

Does technical analysis work? Some of it does, in specific contexts, when applied consistently and tested honestly before you put real money on it. Most of it, used the way most people use it, does not produce a real edge. It produces a compelling story.

The traders I know who trade for a living are not using exotic indicators or complex chart patterns. They are using simple, clearly defined setups. They test those setups. They know what the win rate and average gain look like over time. And when the numbers stop working, they notice and adjust instead of telling themselves a better story.

That is the real takeaway. Technical analysis is a hypothesis. Treat it like one. Write it down, test it, track it, and let the numbers tell you whether it is actually working.

Common questions

Does technical analysis work for day trading?

Some tools do, like session timing and volume-confirmed breakouts. But most individual chart patterns, when tested over many trades, show little consistent edge for day traders. Testing your setup with a journal is the only way to know for sure.

Is technical analysis reliable for predicting price direction?

No single technical tool reliably predicts price direction. What some tools do is identify conditions where a particular outcome happens more often than chance, which is different from prediction and much harder to prove.

Why do so many traders use technical analysis if it does not always work?

Because it feels like it works. Our brains find patterns everywhere, and charts make it easy to build a convincing story after the fact. The feeling of being right is not the same as having a real edge.

What technical analysis tools have the most evidence behind them?

Trend-following rules, volume confirmation, and broad support and resistance zones have the most evidence. Complex multi-indicator systems tend to look great on old charts but fail when applied to new ones.

How do I know if my technical analysis is actually working?

Track every signal your setup generates, wins and losses both, in a trading journal. If your edge is real, the numbers will show it over time. If you only track the trades you take, you are still just telling yourself a story.
