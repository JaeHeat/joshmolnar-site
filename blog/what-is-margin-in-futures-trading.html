What Is Margin in Futures Trading?

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 6 min read

If you want to trade futures, you will hear the word margin before you do almost anything else. It shows up when you open an account, when you place your first trade, and again if a trade goes against you. Most beginners assume margin means the same thing it means in the stock market. It does not. So let me explain what margin in futures trading actually is, why it matters, and what happens when you get it wrong.

Margin in Futures Trading Is Not a Loan

In stocks, margin means you are borrowing money from your broker to buy shares. You pay interest on that loan. Futures margin is completely different. When you trade a futures contract, you are not buying anything. You are making an agreement to buy or sell something at a future date. Margin is simply the deposit your broker holds to make sure both sides of the trade can cover their losses. Think of it like a security deposit on an apartment. You are not paying for the apartment. You are proving you are good for it.

Because futures margin is a deposit and not a loan, you do not pay interest on it. That is one of the reasons futures are popular with active traders.

Initial Margin vs Maintenance Margin

There are two margin numbers you need to know.

  • Initial margin is the amount your broker requires you to have in your account before you can open a trade. The exchange (like the CME) sets a minimum, and your broker can require more. This number changes based on how volatile the market is. When markets are calm, margin tends to be lower. When markets are wild, it goes up.
  • Maintenance margin is the minimum your account must stay above while you hold the position. It is usually a bit less than the initial margin. If your account drops below this level, you get a margin call.

A margin call means your broker is telling you to add money or close the trade. If you do not act, the broker can close it for you. This is not a warning you want to receive, and it is almost always a sign that you sized the trade too large for your account.

How Much Margin Do You Actually Need?

Margin requirements depend on the contract you trade. A full-size E-mini S&P 500 (ES) contract might require thousands of dollars in overnight margin. A Micro E-mini (MES) contract requires a fraction of that, sometimes just a few hundred dollars to hold overnight. Day trading margin, where you close before the session ends, is even lower at most brokers.

Here is the part that trips people up. Just because the margin requirement is low does not mean you should trade with a tiny account. The margin is the minimum to open a position. It says nothing about whether you can survive a normal losing streak on that position. I talk about this a lot in my trading for a living guide. The margin lets you in the door. Your risk per trade is what keeps you alive.

Why Margin Makes Futures So Powerful (and Dangerous)

Futures margin is usually a small percentage of the full contract value, often somewhere between 3 and 12 percent. That means you are controlling a position worth far more than the cash in your account. This is leverage, and it works both ways. A small move in your favor can produce a meaningful profit relative to your deposit. A small move against you can wipe out that same deposit just as fast.

This is exactly why risk management matters more in futures than almost anywhere else. If you size your trades based on how much margin you have instead of how much you can afford to lose, you will eventually blow up. It is not a question of skill. It is math.

Margin and Prop Firm Accounts

If you trade funded prop firm accounts, margin still applies, but the firm usually handles the capital side. Your job is to stay inside the firm’s daily loss and total loss limits. Those limits function like a tighter version of maintenance margin. Break them and you lose the account. The lesson is the same. Knowing what margin is matters less than knowing how to size a trade so one loss never takes you out.

The Simple Version

Margin in futures is a security deposit, not a loan. Your broker holds it to make sure you can pay if the trade goes against you. There are two levels. Initial margin gets you into the trade. Maintenance margin keeps you in it. If your account drops below maintenance, you get a call to add money or get out. The margin requirement is always a fraction of the full contract value, which gives you leverage but also means losses can add up fast. The fix is not to avoid futures. It is to size every trade from your risk, not from your margin. That one habit is the difference between the traders who last and the ones who do not.

Common questions

Is margin in futures the same as margin in stocks?

No. In stocks, margin is a loan from your broker and you pay interest. In futures, margin is a security deposit to cover potential losses. There is no loan and no interest.

What happens if I get a margin call in futures?

Your broker will ask you to deposit more money or close your position. If you do not act quickly, the broker can close the trade for you, often at the worst possible time.

How much margin do I need to trade futures?

It depends on the contract. Micro futures like MES require a few hundred dollars for overnight margin. Full-size contracts like ES require thousands. Day trading margin is lower at most brokers, but low margin does not mean low risk.

Can I lose more than my margin in futures?

Yes. Margin is the minimum deposit, not your maximum loss. In a fast-moving market, your loss can exceed your margin, which is why proper position sizing matters more than the margin number itself.

What is the difference between initial and maintenance margin?

Initial margin is what you need to open the trade. Maintenance margin is the minimum your account must hold while the trade is open. Drop below maintenance and you get a margin call.
