When to Enter a Trade (And When to Wait)

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 6 min read

When to enter a trade is probably the first question every new trader asks. It feels like the whole game. Find the perfect entry, ride it up, cash out. But after years of trading full time, I can tell you that the entry is one of the least important parts of a good trade. Your stop, your size, and your plan matter more. Still, a bad entry can turn a winning idea into a losing trade, so let us talk about how I actually decide when to pull the trigger.

Why most entries fail

The most common mistake I see is entering because you feel like the market is about to move. You see a candle shoot up, you panic that you are going to miss it, and you chase. By the time you are in, the move is already stretched. Your stop has to go far away, which means your position is too small to matter or too big to survive. That is not an entry. That is FOMO with a buy button.

A real entry starts with a plan that existed before the candle printed. If you are reacting to what just happened instead of executing something you already wrote down, you are probably too late.

When to enter a trade using price levels

I look for the market to come to me, not the other way around. That means I identify a level first and then wait for price to reach it. A level could be a support or resistance zone, a prior high or low that the market keeps respecting, or a spot where buyers and sellers clearly fought before.

When price arrives at that level, I watch what it does. Does it reject quickly and snap back? Does it sit there and chop? Does it slice right through? The reaction at the level tells me whether the trade is alive or dead. No reaction, no trade.

Confirmation matters more than prediction

New traders want to predict. They want to call the top or bottom before it happens. Experienced traders wait for confirmation. Confirmation just means the market has shown you evidence that your idea is working before you risk money on it.

For example, if I think a level will hold, I do not buy the instant price touches it. I wait for price to touch, reject, and start moving back in my direction. That small wait costs me a few points on the entry, but it saves me from the times the level breaks and I would have been stopped out immediately. A slightly worse entry with a much higher chance of working is always the better trade.

The checklist I use before every entry

I keep it simple. Before I enter any trade, I need a yes on all of these.

  • Is there a clear level or setup from my trading plan?
  • Has price reached that level and shown a reaction?
  • Do I know exactly where my stop goes before I click?
  • Is the distance from entry to stop small enough that the reward is worth it?
  • Am I calm, or am I chasing because I feel like I am missing something?

If any answer is no, I do not take the trade. That last question is the one most people skip, and it is the one that matters the most.

When to wait instead of entering

Waiting is the hardest skill in trading. It does not feel productive. It does not feel like work. But the best traders I know, including the ones I mentor, spend most of their screen time doing nothing. They are waiting for the market to set up their exact scenario.

Wait when the market is choppy and not respecting any levels. Wait when a big news event is about to drop and you have no edge on the outcome. Wait when you just took a loss and your gut is telling you to win it back right now. That urge to win it back is exactly revenge trading, and it will cost you more than the original loss almost every time.

The goal is not to trade more. The goal is to trade only when the odds favor you. I talk about this a lot when I work with people learning to trade for a living, because the ones who survive are the ones who can sit on their hands.

Entries on prop firm accounts

If you trade funded prop firm accounts, your entries carry extra weight. Every sloppy entry that leads to a stop-out chips away at a limited cushion. You do not get unlimited chances. The trailing floor follows your account up but never comes back down, so a string of bad entries can quietly push you into a spot where one more loss ends the account.

That is why I treat every entry on a funded account the same way. Checklist first. Level, reaction, stop placement, reward worth the risk. No checklist, no trade. Boring keeps the account alive.

The bottom line

When to enter a trade comes down to three things. Have a plan before the market moves. Wait for the market to come to your level. Then confirm the idea is working before you risk anything. The traders who last are not the ones with the best entries. They are the ones who skip the bad ones.

Common questions

How do I know when to enter a trade?

Identify a price level from your trading plan, wait for price to reach it, then look for a reaction that confirms your idea before entering. Never enter just because the market is moving.

What is the best entry point for day trading?

The best entry is one where price has reached a clear level, shown a reaction, and your stop loss is close enough that the potential reward is worth the risk. There is no single perfect entry for every trade.

Should I enter a trade immediately or wait for confirmation?

Wait for confirmation. A slightly worse entry price with evidence the level is holding beats a perfect entry that gets stopped out because the level broke.

How do I stop chasing trades?

Use a checklist before every trade. If the setup was not in your plan before the candle printed, skip it. Chasing means you are reacting to what already happened instead of executing a plan.

When should I not enter a trade?

Do not enter when the market is choppy, right before a major news event, or after a loss when you feel the urge to win it back. Those are the highest-risk, lowest-edge situations.
