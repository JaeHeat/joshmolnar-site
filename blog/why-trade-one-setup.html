Why You Should Only Trade One Setup

By Josh Molnar · October 2026 · 5 min read

The single best piece of advice I give people I mentor is also the one they resist the most. Trade one setup. Not two, not five, not a rotation of whatever looked good on Twitter last week. One. The reason most traders never get consistent is not that they lack a good strategy. It is that they never stick with any strategy long enough to actually learn it.

Why trading one setup works

Think about it like learning a sport. Nobody gets good at basketball by practicing free throws on Monday, tennis serves on Tuesday, and golf swings on Wednesday. You get good by doing the same thing over and over until the movement lives in your hands. Trading is the same. One setup, repeated hundreds of times, teaches you things that no amount of switching ever will.

When you trade one setup, you start to notice the small details that separate the winners from the losers. You see how it behaves in fast markets versus slow ones. You learn which conditions make it work best and which ones to skip. That kind of depth is impossible when you are splitting your attention across five different patterns.

Strategy hopping is the real problem

Most people who say they cannot find a good strategy actually have a different problem. They found one, it lost a few times in a row, and they switched. Then the next one lost, and they switched again. This is strategy hopping, and it is one of the most common reasons traders never improve. Every time you switch, you reset the learning clock back to zero.

I have tested dozens of strategies with real data. Most of them have losing streaks built right into their history. That is normal. If you bail after three or four losses, you will never stick with anything long enough to see the wins that follow. I talk about this more in my guide on trading for a living, because surviving those stretches is what separates people who make it from people who quit.

What happens when you commit

When you lock in on one setup, a few things change immediately.

  • Your decisions get simpler. Either the setup is there or it is not. You take it or you wait. That clarity removes most of the emotional noise that causes bad trades.
  • Your journal becomes useful. When every trade in your log is the same type, you can actually spot patterns. You can see which days, times, or conditions produce the best results. When your log has five different setups mixed together, it is just noise.
  • You stop overtrading. Half the reason people take too many trades is that they have too many setups on their screen. Fewer options means fewer bad decisions. I wrote about this pattern in detail in my post on overtrading.

How to pick your one setup

The setup itself matters less than you think. What matters is that you can define it clearly, that you have tested it, and that you actually follow it. Here is a simple filter.

  1. Write down the exact conditions for entry, stop, and target. If you cannot describe it in plain sentences, it is not defined enough.
  2. Test it. Go back through historical charts and count at least 50 examples. Look at the wins, the losses, and the overall result. If the numbers do not work across a real sample, pick a different one.
  3. Trade it on a simulator or small size for at least a month. Track every trade. Review weekly.

If you trade funded prop firm accounts, this matters even more. Prop firms have strict rules about daily losses and overall account limits. You cannot afford to be experimenting with five setups on a funded account. One tested setup with fixed risk is how you stay inside those rules.

When to add a second setup

You earn the right to add a second setup only after you have traded the first one profitably over a large sample. I am talking months, not days. At that point your first setup is automatic. You do not have to think about the rules because you have done it so many times. Only then does adding complexity make sense.

Most traders never need more than two or three setups. The best traders I know personally trade fewer setups than beginners, not more. The edge is in the depth of understanding, not the width of the playbook.

The boring truth

Trading one setup is boring. There will be days, sometimes weeks, where your setup simply does not show up and you sit on your hands. That is the job. The alternative is forcing trades that do not fit your rules, and that is the fast track to losing money. If you want to build a real, repeatable process, boring is the price of admission. I go deeper on this in my guide to day trading crypto, where the temptation to overtrade is even stronger because the market never closes.

Common questions

How many setups should a beginner trader have?

One. Master a single setup over hundreds of trades before adding anything else. Trying to learn multiple setups at once splits your focus and slows your progress.

What is strategy hopping in trading?

Strategy hopping means switching to a new strategy every time the current one has a few losses. It resets your learning and prevents you from ever developing real skill with any single approach.

How long should you trade one setup before adding another?

Trade one setup for at least a few months and across a large enough sample of trades that the results are meaningful. Only add a second setup once the first one is consistent and feels automatic.

Does trading one setup limit how much money you can make?

No. Depth beats width. One setup you truly understand and execute well will almost always outperform five setups you half-know. The money comes from execution quality, not from having more strategies.
