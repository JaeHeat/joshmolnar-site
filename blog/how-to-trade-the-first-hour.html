How to Trade the First Hour of the Day

By Josh Molnar · October 2026 · 6 min read

The first hour of trading is the loudest, fastest, most chaotic part of the day. It is also where the biggest moves happen. Roughly 25 to 30 percent of the entire day’s volume gets crammed into that first 60 minutes after the opening bell. If you do not have a plan for the first hour, you are gambling during the one window that matters most.

I am going to walk you through how I actually approach it. No magic formula. Just a repeatable process that keeps you from chasing and gives you a reason to be in or out.

Why the First Hour of Trading Is Different

Overnight news, earnings, economic reports, and global markets all collide at the open. That is why the first hour produces bigger candles, wider spreads, and faster fills than any other part of the session. The good news is that big moves mean big opportunity. The bad news is that big moves also mean big losses if you are not ready.

Most of the traders I mentor make the same mistake. They stare at the screen the moment the bell rings, see a candle shoot up, and jump in with no plan. Two minutes later the candle reverses, their stop gets hit, and they spend the rest of the day trying to win it back. That cycle is what I call the open trap, and it is the number one reason beginners bleed money during the first hour.

Do Your Homework Before the Open

The first hour is won or lost in the 30 minutes before the bell. Here is what I look at every single morning.

  • Where did the market close yesterday? Where is it trading in the pre-market? A gap up or gap down changes the plan.
  • Are there any major economic releases today? The economic calendar tells you whether to expect fireworks or a slow grind.
  • What are the key levels from yesterday? The prior day’s high, low, and close are the first lines on my chart every morning.

If I do not know these three things before 9:30, I do not trade the open. Period.

Wait for the Opening Range to Form

This is the part most people skip. Instead of trading the very first candle, I let the first 15 to 30 minutes play out and mark the high and low of that window. That range is called the opening range, and it gives you two things you did not have at 9:30. You now have a clear level to trade above. And you have a clear level to trade below. Without those, you are just guessing.

The opening range works because it absorbs the chaos. All the overnight orders, the panic sellers, the gap chasers, they all fire during those first minutes. Once the dust settles, what is left is a range that tells you where buyers stepped in and where sellers stepped in. That is real information.

Trade the Break, Not the Chop

Once the opening range is set, I wait for price to break above the high or below the low on strong volume. That breakout is my signal. If it breaks above, I look for a long entry. If it breaks below, I look short. If it stays inside the range, I do nothing. Sitting out is a trade too.

The key is that your stop goes on the other side of the range. You know exactly what you are risking before you click the button. That is how professional traders stay in the game. They never enter a trade without knowing the exit first.

Manage the Trade, Do Not Marry It

First-hour breakouts move fast. That is their gift and their curse. I take partial profits early and move my stop to breakeven as soon as the trade gives me room. If the breakout stalls or reverses back into the range, I am out. No questions, no hoping, no averaging down.

The biggest trap is holding a first-hour winner too long. By 10:30 or 11:00 the volume dries up, the moves get smaller, and what felt like a rocket turns into a slow leak. I would rather book a solid gain in the first hour and walk away than give it all back in the dead zone that follows.

What to Avoid in the First Hour

A short list of things that cost me money before I learned to stop doing them.

  • Trading the first candle. It is almost always a trap. Let the range form.
  • Chasing a move that already happened. If you missed it, you missed it. There will be another one.
  • Oversizing because it “looks obvious.” The first hour is volatile. Volatile means your stop can get hit faster than you expect. Risk the same small amount you would on any other trade.
  • Ignoring your plan because of FOMO. The open is designed to make you feel like you are missing out. That feeling is not information. Your plan is.

The Bottom Line

The first hour of trading is the best window of the day if you have a process and the worst if you do not. Do your homework before the open. Let the opening range form. Trade the breakout with a defined stop. Take profits early. And when the volume dies, stop trading. That is it. It is not exciting, and that is exactly the point. If you want to build a real day trading process around this, start by watching three to five opens without placing a single trade. Mark the range, track the breakouts, and see for yourself how often the pattern repeats. The screen time will teach you more than any article can.

Common questions

What is the best time to day trade?

The first hour after the market opens, roughly 9:30 to 10:30 AM Eastern, consistently has the highest volume and largest price moves. Most professional day traders do the bulk of their work in this window.

Should you trade right at the market open?

Not usually. The very first minutes are chaotic and filled with overnight orders firing at once. Letting the first 15 to 30 minutes form an opening range gives you defined levels to trade against instead of guessing.

What is the opening range in trading?

The opening range is the high and low of the first 15 to 30 minutes after the market opens. Traders use it as a reference point. A breakout above the high or below the low often signals the direction for the rest of the session.

Why does volume drop after the first hour?

Most institutional and overnight orders get filled in the first hour. Once that wave passes, fewer participants are active, which means smaller moves and choppier price action until the final hour of the day.

How many trades should you take in the first hour?

One or two quality setups is enough. The goal is not to trade as much as possible. It is to wait for a clean breakout with a defined stop and take only the highest-quality opportunity.
