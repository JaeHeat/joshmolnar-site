Best Candlestick Patterns for Day Trading

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 5 min read

When I first started day trading, I thought candlestick patterns were the whole game. Learn enough patterns, see the signal, take the trade. It took blowing up two accounts to understand what actually matters. The best candlestick patterns for day trading are not magic signals. They are visual clues about who is winning in a small stretch of time, and they only make sense when you read them in context.

What makes a candlestick pattern worth learning

A single candle shows you four things. The open, the high, the low, and the close. The body tells you where price started and ended. The wicks tell you where it went and got rejected. A pattern worth learning tells a simple story. Buyers pushed up hard and sellers stopped them. Or sellers dumped hard and buyers stepped in. The best patterns show that story clearly, without you having to squint.

The ones below are the patterns I actually watch. Not because they always work, but because the story they tell is easy to read and they show up often enough to matter.

The hammer

A hammer has a small body near the top of the candle and a long wick below it. Price fell hard during that period, then buyers came in and pushed it back up before the candle closed. The long lower wick is the key detail. It tells you sellers tried to push lower and failed.

I look for hammers at obvious support levels, the kind that any trader watching the chart can see. A hammer floating in the middle of nowhere tells you nothing useful. At a level where price has bounced before, it tells you buyers showed up right where you expected them to.

The engulfing candle

A bullish engulfing candle is two candles in a row. The first is red (sellers won). The second is green and bigger, completely covering the first candle. It tells you that whatever selling pressure existed got absorbed and reversed in a single period. The buyers won decisively.

The bearish version works the same way in the opposite direction. A big green candle followed by a bigger red one that swallows it.

Engulfing candles are some of the most reliable-looking signals in price action, and they still require context. An engulfing candle means the most when it forms near a support or resistance level that has held multiple times. Away from those levels, I treat it as noise.

The inside bar

An inside bar is a small candle that fits completely within the range of the candle before it. The high is lower than the previous high. The low is higher than the previous low. It means the market paused. Neither buyers nor sellers took control.

Traders watch inside bars because a pause after a strong move often signals compression before the next move. It is not a signal by itself. It is a sign to watch more closely. I use it to spot where a breakout might come from, not to predict which direction it will go.

The doji

A doji opens and closes at nearly the same price, leaving a candle with almost no body and wicks on both sides. Buyers and sellers fought for the whole period and ended right where they started. Neither side won.

A doji on its own is not a trade signal. It is a question mark. It says the previous trend might be losing steam. For a trade, I want to see what happens next. A doji at resistance followed by a red candle is interesting. A doji in the middle of a trending market, away from any key level, is usually just noise.

How to actually use these patterns

The mistake most beginners make is pattern-hunting. They scroll a chart looking for hammers and engulfing candles without first asking where they are on the chart. I do the opposite. I find the levels first, then I watch what the candles do when price gets there.

Start with a clear process for reading the market before you lean on patterns. If you are trading crypto, you can see what that looks like in my guide to day trading crypto. Know your key levels. Know whether the market is trending or sitting still. Then use candlestick patterns as the trigger that says “price got to that level AND buyers showed up” rather than just one or the other.

If you are still learning how candles are built from the ground up, I walk through how to read candlestick charts from scratch. That foundation makes everything above click faster.

Patterns do not predict the future. They compress recent information into a visual form that is fast to read under pressure. That is their real value. Keep them simple, use them in context, and they become a useful part of building a process that holds up over time.

Common questions

What is the best candlestick pattern for day trading?

There is no single best pattern. The engulfing candle and the hammer are two of the clearest signals because they show a decisive shift in who is in control, but any pattern only matters when it forms at a meaningful price level.

Do candlestick patterns actually work in day trading?

They work as one part of a process, not as standalone signals. A pattern at a key support or resistance level gives you useful information. The same pattern in the middle of a range usually does not.

How many candlestick patterns do I need to know?

Most experienced traders rely on three to five patterns they know well rather than memorizing dozens. Learning fewer patterns deeply, and understanding when to trust them, is more useful than recognizing every pattern that exists.

What is the difference between a hammer and an engulfing candle?

A hammer is a single candle with a long lower wick showing buyers rejected a push lower. An engulfing candle is two candles where the second completely covers the first, showing a full reversal of the previous move.

Can I trade using only candlestick patterns?

Most traders who try this lose money. Patterns need context, specifically key price levels and an understanding of the broader trend, to be useful. Without that context they are just shapes on a screen.
