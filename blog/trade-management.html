Trade Management: What to Do After You Enter

By Josh Molnar · October 2026 · 6 min read

Most traders spend all their energy on the entry. They study patterns, test indicators, wait for the perfect setup. Then they get in and have no idea what to do next. Should I move my stop? Should I take profit here? Should I hold? That confusion is where most accounts bleed out. Trade management is the part of trading that happens after you click the button, and it is the part that actually decides whether you make money.

What is trade management?

Trade management is everything you do between entering a trade and closing it. That includes where your stop loss sits, when you move it, where you plan to take profit, whether you take partial profits along the way, and what price action would make you exit early. Think of the entry as opening a door. Trade management is everything you do inside the room.

Write the plan before you enter

The single biggest trade management mistake is making decisions while the trade is live. When you are watching your profit or loss change every second, your brain is not making rational choices. It is reacting to fear and greed. The fix is boring and effective. Write out three things before every trade:

  • Where does your stop loss go? This is the price that proves your idea wrong.
  • Where is your first target? This is the level where you plan to take some or all profit.
  • What would make you exit early, even if your stop has not been hit?

If you cannot answer those three questions, you do not have a trade. You have a guess. I break down the full pre-trade process in my guide on day trading crypto, and it applies to any market.

Stop loss management after entry

Your initial stop is set before you enter. After entry, the only rule is this. Never widen it. Moving your stop further away to give the trade more room is not management. It is hoping, and hoping is how you turn a small planned loss into a catastrophe.

The question most people ask is when to move the stop closer. A common approach is to move to breakeven once the trade has moved roughly one times your risk in your favor. So if you risked 100 dollars, and the trade is up 100 dollars, you slide the stop to your entry price. Now the worst case is zero loss instead of a full loss. But there is a trap. If you move to breakeven too early on a choppy setup, you get stopped out on noise and miss the real move. Match your breakeven rule to the setup. A tight scalp can move fast. A wider swing needs more room.

Taking profits without leaving money on the table

Here is the tension every trader lives with. Take profit too early and you cap your winners. Hold too long and you watch a winner turn into a loser. There is no perfect answer, but there are frameworks that keep you sane.

One approach is partial profits. You close half your position at the first target, then let the rest run with a trailing stop. The first half locks in a win. The second half gives you a shot at the bigger move. Even if the trailer gets stopped out at breakeven, you still walk away with something.

Another approach is all-or-nothing. You set a fixed target before entry and either hit it or get stopped out. This is simpler and avoids the mental gymnastics of managing a partial, but it does mean you will watch winners come back to your stop sometimes.

Neither is wrong. What is wrong is having no plan at all and making it up while the candle is moving. I go deeper on exit strategy in how to take profits in day trading.

The emotional traps that wreck your management

Every management mistake traces back to the same two feelings. Fear makes you close winners too early because you are scared the profit will disappear. Greed makes you hold losers too long because you are sure it will come back. The result is the exact opposite of what works. You cut winners short and let losers run.

The fix is mechanical rules. Not guidelines, not vibes. Rules you wrote down before the trade opened. When you follow the plan, your emotions become irrelevant. That does not mean you stop feeling them. It means the plan acts on your behalf while your brain is panicking.

If you want to trade for a living, this is the skill that gets you there. Not finding better entries. Managing the ones you already have.

A simple trade management checklist

Before you take your next trade, answer each of these:

  1. Is my stop loss placed at a level that invalidates the setup, not just a round number I picked?
  2. Do I have a first profit target written down?
  3. Do I know exactly what happens when price reaches that target (close all, close half, trail)?
  4. Is there a condition that would make me exit early, even before my stop is hit?
  5. Have I written all of this down before I enter?

If you said yes to all five, you have a managed trade. If you said no to any of them, you are gambling with a chart open.

The bottom line

Your entry is maybe 10 percent of the trade. The other 90 percent is what you do after you are in. Trade management is not glamorous. Nobody posts their trailing stop rules on social media. But it is the difference between a trader who survives and one who blows up doing the exact same setups. Write the plan, follow the plan, and let the plan do the hard work while your emotions try to sabotage you.

Common questions

What is trade management in trading?

Trade management is everything you do between entering a trade and closing it. It includes your stop loss placement, when you move it, where you take profit, and what conditions would make you exit early.

When should I move my stop loss to breakeven?

A common rule is to move to breakeven once the trade has moved roughly one times your risk in your favor. But the right timing depends on the setup. Tight scalps can move fast, while wider swings need more room to breathe.

Should I take partial profits or all at once?

Both work. Partial profits lock in a win while letting the rest run. All-or-nothing is simpler and avoids over-managing. The only wrong answer is having no plan and deciding in the moment.

Why do I keep cutting winners short?

Fear of losing unrealized profit makes most traders close too early. The fix is a written exit plan made before you enter, so the decision is mechanical instead of emotional.

How do I stop making emotional decisions during a trade?

Write your stop, target, and exit rules before you enter. Follow them exactly. You will still feel the urge to override the plan, but if the rules are written down and tested, the plan is smarter than your panic.
