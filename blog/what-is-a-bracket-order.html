What Is a Bracket Order? Entry, Stop and Target in One

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 6 min read

When I first started trading, my routine was a mess. I would click buy, then rush to place my stop loss, then try to remember where I wanted to take profit. Some days the price moved before I finished typing. Some days I skipped the stop completely and told myself I would watch it. A bracket order fixes all of that. So what is a bracket order? It is one order that carries three instructions at once. It says where you get in, where you get out if you are wrong, and where you get out if you are right.

What a bracket order is, in plain English

Think of it as a trade with seatbelts already buckled. A bracket order has three parts.

  • The entry. This is the order that opens the trade.
  • The stop loss. This is the exit that closes the trade for a small loss if the price goes against you.
  • The profit target. This is the exit that closes the trade for a gain if the price goes your way.

The name comes from the picture it makes on the chart. If you buy, your target sits above your entry and your stop sits below it. The two exits bracket your entry price, one on each side.

How a bracket order works step by step

Here is what happens after you send one.

  1. Your entry order goes out first. The two exits wait in the background. They do nothing until you are actually in the trade.
  2. Once the entry fills, the stop and the target switch on together.
  3. The price reaches one of them. That exit fills and closes the trade.
  4. The other exit is cancelled automatically.

That last step matters more than it looks. The two exits are linked. Traders call this link one cancels the other, or OCO for short. Without it, a leftover order could sit in the market after your trade is done and open a brand new trade you never wanted.

A simple example helps. Say I buy at 100. I set my stop at 98 and my target at 104. I am risking 2 to try to make 4. If the price hits 104 first, I am out with a gain and the stop at 98 disappears. If the price hits 98 first, I am out with a small loss and the target disappears. Either way, I did not have to touch anything. If the idea of risking 2 to make 4 is new to you, I explain where that stop should go in how to set a stop loss.

Why I use a bracket order on every trade

The honest reason is that I do not trust myself in the middle of a trade. Nobody should. Before the trade, I am calm and I can think. During the trade, my money is moving and my brain starts making excuses. A bracket order lets the calm version of me make the decisions, and it locks them in before the nervous version shows up.

It also means I am never in a trade without protection, not even for a few seconds. If my internet drops or the market jumps, the stop is already there. That matters on any account, and it matters even more on funded prop firm accounts, where one oversized loss can break the rules and end the account that day.

The last benefit is a quiet one. A bracket order forces me to answer two questions before I click. Where am I wrong? Where am I done? If I cannot answer both, I do not have a trade. I just have a feeling.

What a bracket order cannot do

It is a tool, not a strategy. Here are the limits I want you to know.

  • It does not make a bad trade good. If your stop and target are in the wrong places, the bracket will carry out a bad plan very neatly.
  • Your stop can fill at a worse price. In a fast market, the price can skip past your stop level before your order fills. That gap is called slippage, and no order type removes it.
  • You can still sabotage it. Most platforms let you drag the stop further away after you are in. The bracket only helps if you leave it alone.
  • Platforms handle it differently. The link between the two exits is usually managed by your broker or your trading software. Practice on a demo account first and learn how yours behaves before real money is on the line.

How to start using one

Almost every modern trading platform has bracket orders. Sometimes they have a different name, such as attached orders or a trade template. Set one up with your normal stop distance and your normal target, save it, and place a few practice trades. Check that both exits appear the moment you get in, and that one vanishes when the other fills.

After that, make it a rule. No bracket, no trade. It will not make you profitable by itself, and most traders still lose money. What it does is remove one of the most common ways people hurt themselves, which is entering a trade with no plan for getting out. Building habits like this one is a big part of what it takes to treat trading for a living as a real job instead of a gamble.

Common questions

What is a bracket order in trading?

A bracket order is a single order with three parts, an entry, a stop loss and a profit target. The two exits switch on once you are in the trade, and when one fills the other is cancelled.

What is the difference between a bracket order and an OCO order?

OCO means one cancels the other, and it links two orders so that filling one removes the other. A bracket order is an entry order with an OCO pair of exits attached to it.

Does a bracket order guarantee my stop loss price?

No. In a fast market the price can jump past your stop before the order fills, so you can get a worse price than you planned. That is called slippage and no order type removes it.

Can I change a bracket order after I enter the trade?

Most platforms let you move the stop or the target after you are in. The bracket only protects you if you leave the stop where your plan put it.

Should beginners use bracket orders?

I think so, because it means you are never in a trade without a stop loss. Practice with one on a demo account first so you know how your platform handles it.
