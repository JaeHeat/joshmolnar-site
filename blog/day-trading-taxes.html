Day Trading Taxes Explained Simply

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 6 min read

Day trading taxes are the part of this job nobody wants to talk about. I get it. You spent months building a process, finally started making money, and now Uncle Sam wants a cut. But here is the thing I tell every trader I mentor. If you do not understand how your profits are taxed, you do not actually know how much you made. And surprises at tax time can undo months of good trading.

I am not a tax professional and nothing here is tax advice. But as someone who trades for a living across crypto and futures, I have had to learn this stuff the hard way. Let me walk you through the basics in plain English so you know what to expect and what to ask your accountant.

How Day Trading Profits Are Taxed

When you buy and sell something within the same year, the profit is a short-term capital gain. Short-term gains are taxed at your ordinary income tax rate. In 2026, that means anywhere from 10 to 37 percent at the federal level, depending on how much total income you earned. That is the same rate you pay on a regular paycheck. There is no special discount for being a trader.

This catches a lot of new traders off guard. They see a 20,000 dollar profit on their brokerage statement and think they have 20,000 dollars to spend. They do not. Depending on their tax bracket, they might owe 5,000 to 7,000 dollars of that to the IRS, plus state taxes on top. The traders who plan for this survive. The ones who spend the whole thing get a nasty bill in April.

Why Futures Traders Get a Tax Break

If you trade futures (like NQ, ES, or micro contracts), there is a meaningful tax advantage you should know about. Futures fall under something called Section 1256, which gives them a 60/40 split. That means 60 percent of your gains are taxed at the lower long-term rate and 40 percent at the short-term rate, no matter how long you held the trade. Even if you opened and closed it in two minutes.

The top blended rate under this rule works out to roughly 27 percent instead of 37 percent. On a 50,000 dollar year, that difference is real money. This is one of the reasons I trade futures through prop firm accounts, and it is worth asking your accountant whether your trading qualifies.

Crypto Taxes Are Different

Crypto is treated as property by the IRS, not as a security. Every time you sell, swap, or close a leveraged crypto position, that is a taxable event. Short-term crypto gains are taxed at ordinary income rates, just like stocks.

One thing that currently works in crypto traders’ favor. The wash sale rule, which prevents stock traders from selling at a loss and immediately rebuying to claim the tax deduction, does not apply to crypto as of 2026. That means if you close a losing crypto trade, you can buy back in right away and still use that loss to offset other gains. Congress has proposed changing this, so it may not last forever. But for now, it is a real advantage. Talk to your accountant about it.

Day Trading Taxes on a Prop Firm Account

If you trade a funded prop firm account, you are not trading your own capital, but you are still responsible for taxes on the payouts you receive. Most prop firms issue a 1099 for your payouts. Whether that income is classified as self-employment income or capital gains depends on the firm and your situation. This is one area where a tax professional who understands trading is worth every dollar.

I talk more about the payout structure in how prop firm payouts work, but the tax side is something you need to sort out before your first payout, not after.

Three Mistakes That Cost Traders at Tax Time

  • Not setting money aside. A simple rule. Every time you take a payout or withdraw a profit, move 30 percent into a separate account for taxes. It is not yours to spend.
  • Ignoring wash sales. If you trade stocks or ETFs and you sell a loser then rebuy within 30 days, the IRS disallows that loss. Your broker tracks this, but many traders do not understand it until they owe more than expected.
  • Not tracking trades. Your broker provides statements, but if you trade across multiple accounts or platforms, keeping your own records matters. A simple spreadsheet or a dedicated tax tool makes filing far less painful.

What to Do Right Now

You do not need to become a tax expert. You need to do three things. First, understand that short-term trading profits are taxed at your full income rate. Second, set aside roughly 30 percent of every profit withdrawal for taxes. Third, find an accountant who works with traders specifically. The money you pay them will save you more than it costs, every single year.

Trading is already hard enough. Do not let a tax bill you did not plan for be the thing that takes you out. Handle this part like you handle risk management. Boring, mechanical, non-negotiable.

Common questions

How are day trading profits taxed?

Day trading profits are short-term capital gains, taxed at your ordinary income tax rate. In 2026, federal rates range from 10 to 37 percent depending on your total income.

Do futures traders pay less in taxes?

Yes. Futures qualify for Section 1256 treatment, which taxes 60 percent of gains at the lower long-term rate and 40 percent at the short-term rate. The top blended rate is roughly 27 percent instead of 37 percent.

Do you pay taxes on prop firm payouts?

Yes. Prop firm payouts are taxable income. Most firms issue a 1099 form. How the income is classified depends on the firm and your situation, so consult a tax professional.

Does the wash sale rule apply to crypto?

As of 2026, no. The IRS treats crypto as property, not a security, so the wash sale rule does not currently apply. This may change if pending legislation passes.

How much should I set aside for trading taxes?

A safe rule of thumb is 30 percent of every profit withdrawal. The actual amount depends on your tax bracket and state, so work with an accountant who understands trading.
