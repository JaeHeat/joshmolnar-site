How to Choose a Trading Strategy

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 5 min read

How to choose a trading strategy is one of the first questions every new trader asks, and almost everyone gets it backwards. They search for the best strategy. They watch ten YouTube videos, try three different setups in the same week, blow up, and decide trading does not work. The problem was never the strategy. It was the fit.

I have tested dozens of strategies over the years. The ones that survived were never the flashiest. They were the ones that matched my schedule, my personality, and my risk rules so well that I could actually follow them on the worst days. That is the filter most people skip.

Why choosing a trading strategy matters more than the strategy itself

Research consistently shows that most day traders lose money. A commonly cited study of Brazilian day traders found that only about 3 percent were profitable after a full year. The losing majority are not all using bad strategies. Many of them are using fine strategies they cannot stick to. They size too big, skip the stop, or abandon the plan after two losses. All of those mistakes come from a mismatch between the trader and the method.

A strategy you can follow on your worst day is worth more than a strategy that looks perfect on paper but falls apart the moment you feel pressure. That is the whole point of choosing carefully.

Three questions to ask before you pick anything

Before you look at a single chart pattern, answer these honestly:

  • How much time do you actually have? If you work a full-time job and can only check charts for an hour in the evening, a method that requires watching every candle for six hours is not going to work. Match the timeframe to your life first.
  • How do you handle losing? Some people can take five small losses in a row and stay calm. Others spiral after two. If losing streaks wreck you emotionally, a strategy that wins less often but wins bigger when it does will grind you down no matter how good the math is.
  • Do you prefer rules or feel? Be honest. Some people thrive with rigid checklists. Others need a little room to read the situation. Neither is wrong, but picking a method that clashes with how your brain works guarantees you will break your own rules.

Start with one strategy and give it a real test

The biggest trap is strategy hopping. You try a breakout method on Monday, switch to a pullback method on Wednesday, add an indicator on Friday, and by the end of the month you have no data on whether any of them work. I wrote a whole piece on why trading one setup changes everything, and it applies here too.

Pick one approach. Trade it in a simulator or on paper for at least 30 to 50 trades. Write down every entry, exit, and result. After those reps you will have real numbers, not opinions, and you can decide whether to keep it, adjust it, or move on.

What a good strategy looks like for a beginner

A good first strategy has a few things in common:

  • Simple, clear rules you can write on an index card. If you cannot explain when to enter, where to place your stop, and when to exit in two sentences, the strategy is too complicated for right now.
  • A defined stop loss on every trade. No strategy is worth anything if it does not tell you exactly where you are wrong. I cover this in detail in trading for a living because it is the one non-negotiable.
  • Enough trades per week to learn. A method that triggers once a month will take years before you have enough experience to judge it. For most beginners, something that gives you a few setups per week on a 15-minute to 1-hour chart is a practical starting point.

Common categories of strategies

You do not need to understand every style right now. Just know the broad categories so you can narrow your search:

  • Breakout strategies look for price pushing above or below a defined range. They tend to win less often but catch big moves when they work.
  • Pullback strategies wait for a strong move, then enter when price temporarily dips back. They feel more comfortable because you are buying at a better price, but they require patience.
  • Mean reversion strategies bet that price will snap back toward an average after stretching too far. These work well in choppy, sideways markets and poorly in trending ones.

None of these is objectively better. The right one depends on your answers to the three questions above and on the market you trade. A breakout approach in day trading crypto looks different from a breakout approach in index futures, even though the idea is the same.

How to know the strategy is working

After your test period, look at two things. First, did you follow the rules on at least 80 percent of your trades? If not, the strategy might not fit you, and no amount of tweaking will fix a fit problem. Second, over 30 or more trades, are you at least close to breakeven or slightly positive? You are not looking for perfection. You are looking for a signal that the method has some real advantage when you execute it properly.

If both check out, you have something worth building on. Keep trading it, keep journaling, and refine the edges. If either fails, go back to the three questions, pick a different category, and test again.

The bottom line

Choosing a trading strategy is not about finding the holy grail. It is about finding the method that fits your life, your personality, and your risk tolerance well enough that you can follow it when everything feels wrong. Pick one, test it with real reps, and let the numbers tell you the truth. That process is slow and boring. It is also the only one that works.

Common questions

What is the best trading strategy for beginners?

There is no single best strategy. The best one for you is the simplest method that matches your available time, your personality, and your risk rules. Start with one approach, test it for at least 30 trades on paper, and let the results decide.

How do I know if my trading strategy works?

After 30 or more trades, check two things. Did you follow the rules at least 80 percent of the time, and are your results close to breakeven or better? If yes, you have a foundation to build on. If not, the method may not fit you.

How many trading strategies should I learn?

Start with one. Trading one setup long enough to get real data is far more valuable than testing five setups for a few days each. You can always add a second method later once the first one is solid.

Why do traders keep switching strategies?

Usually because they chose a strategy that does not match their schedule, personality, or risk tolerance. When a method does not fit, every losing trade feels like proof that the strategy is broken, even when the losses are normal.
