What Is a Futures Rollover? A Plain English Guide

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 6 min read

The first time I saw it, I thought my chart was broken. I opened my platform one morning and the price looked like it had jumped overnight, even though nothing big had happened in the news. Nothing was broken. I had just run into a futures rollover for the first time. A futures rollover is the moment traders move from a contract that is about to end to the next one in line. It sounds technical. It is really just a calendar event, and once you understand it, it stops being scary.

Why futures contracts have an end date

A share of stock can live forever. A futures contract cannot. Every contract comes with an end date built in, and after that date it stops trading. I cover the basics of this in what a futures contract is.

Think of it like a carton of milk. The milk is fine today, but there is a date printed on the side. The store does not stop selling milk when that carton gets old. It just puts a fresh carton on the shelf with a later date. Futures work the same way. There is always a fresh contract waiting behind the old one.

For the big US stock index futures, the contracts end four times a year, on the third Friday of March, June, September, and December. Other markets, like oil or gold, run on their own calendars.

What is a futures rollover, step by step?

Here is the whole thing in three steps.

  1. A contract gets close to its end date.
  2. Traders start closing their trades in the old contract and opening them in the next one.
  3. Within a few days, almost everyone is trading the new contract and the old one goes quiet.

That move from old to new is the rollover. For stock index futures, most of the crowd makes the switch in the week or so before the end date. They do not wait for the last day.

Why does the crowd matter? Because you want to trade where the other traders are. A busy contract has lots of buyers and sellers, so your orders get filled quickly and at fair prices. A contract that is about to end turns into an empty room. Fewer people are there, and getting in and out gets harder. I explain why a busy market helps you in what volume is in trading.

Why the price looks different after a futures rollover

This is the part that fooled me. The old contract and the new contract are two separate things, and they do not trade at the exact same price. The new one might sit a little higher or a little lower.

So when your chart switches from the old contract to the new one, the price can look like it jumped. It did not really jump. Nobody made or lost money on that gap. You are simply looking at a different carton of milk with a different price tag.

Many charting platforms smooth this out for you. They stitch the old and new contracts into one long chart and slide the old prices up or down so the line connects. That is helpful, but it has a side effect. The old price levels on that chart may not match the prices that really traded back then. If a line you drew last month suddenly looks off after a rollover, this is usually why.

What a day trader should actually do

I day trade, so I am out of my trades before the session ends. That means I never hold a contract to its end date. For me, the rollover comes down to a short routine.

  • Know the dates. The exchange publishes the calendar. I mark the rollover weeks so they never surprise me.
  • Follow the crowd. When most of the trading has moved to the new contract, I switch my chart and my order window to it.
  • Check which contract you are in. Before the first trade of the day, I look at the contract name on my order window. Placing a trade in the old, quiet contract is an easy mistake to make.
  • Redraw your levels. After the switch, I check my key price levels on the new contract instead of trusting the old lines.
  • Expect a messier week. With traders split between two contracts, price can act a little strange. I would rather trade smaller or sit out than force it.

If you trade a funded account, there is one more step. Brokers and firms can set their own cutoff for when you must be out of the old contract, and it may come earlier than the exchange date. Read those rules before rollover week, not during it. Small rule slips like this are one of the things I walk through in my guide to prop firm trading.

The bottom line

A futures rollover is not a trading signal and it is not a glitch. It is housekeeping. The old contract is ending, the crowd moves to the next one, and your job is to move with them and double check your chart. None of this makes trading safer or easier. Most traders still lose money, and knowing the calendar will not change that. It just removes one avoidable mistake, and removing avoidable mistakes is most of what a good process is.

Common questions

What is a futures rollover in simple terms?

It is when traders move from a futures contract that is about to end to the next one in line. The market keeps going, but the trading shifts to a fresh contract with a later end date.

When do stock index futures roll over?

The big US stock index futures end on the third Friday of March, June, September, and December. Most traders switch to the next contract in the week or so before that date.

Why does the price jump on my chart at a futures rollover?

The old and new contracts are separate and trade at slightly different prices. When your chart switches from one to the other, it can look like a jump even though nobody made or lost money on that gap.

Do day traders need to worry about futures rollover?

A little. Day traders close their trades before the session ends, so they never hold to the end date, but they still need to switch to the new contract when the crowd does and recheck their price levels.

What happens if I trade the old contract after the rollover?

Fewer traders are left in it, so getting in and out can be harder and prices can be worse. Your broker or firm may also have its own cutoff for when you must be out of the old contract.
