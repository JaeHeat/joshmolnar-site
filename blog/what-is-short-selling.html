What Is Short Selling? How Betting on a Drop Works

By Josh Molnar · November 2026 · 6 min read

Most people think you can only make money in a market when the price goes up. You buy low, you sell high, and that is the whole game. Then they hear a trader say “I am short” and it sounds like a magic trick. So what is short selling? It is a bet that a price will fall. That is all it is. The idea is simple, but the risk works differently from buying, and that difference catches a lot of new traders off guard.

What is short selling, in one picture?

Normal trading goes in this order. You buy first, then you sell later. Short selling flips the order. You sell first, then you buy later.

Think of it like this. Your friend owns a video game that sells for 50 dollars today. You are sure the price is about to drop. So you borrow the game from your friend and sell it to someone for 50 dollars. A week later the store marks it down to 40 dollars. You buy a new copy for 40, hand it back to your friend, and keep the 10 dollars left over.

Your friend got the game back. You made money because the price fell.

How short selling works with stocks

With stocks, the friend is your broker. The steps look like this.

  1. You borrow shares from your broker.
  2. You sell those shares right away at today’s price.
  3. Later, you buy the same number of shares back.
  4. You return them to the broker.

Say you borrow 100 shares and sell them at 50 dollars each. That is 5,000 dollars. If the price falls to 40 dollars, you buy them back for 4,000 dollars and the 1,000 dollar difference is your gain, before costs. If the price rises to 60 dollars instead, you have to pay 6,000 dollars to buy them back. Now you are down 1,000 dollars.

There are costs too. Brokers usually charge a fee for lending you the shares. And sometimes there are simply no shares available to borrow, so the trade cannot be made at all.

How going short works in futures

This is where I spend most of my time, and it is simpler. In futures there is nothing to borrow. A futures contract is just an agreement between a buyer and a seller. To go short, you take the seller’s side. You click sell instead of buy. Later you click buy to close the trade.

Going short in futures is as easy as going long. That is one reason so many day traders use them, and it is why short trades are a normal part of trading prop firm accounts.

Why a short trade is riskier than it looks

When you buy something, there is a floor under you. A price cannot go below zero. If you buy a stock at 50 dollars, the worst case is that it goes to zero and you lose 50 dollars a share. That is painful, but it has an end.

When you short something, there is no ceiling over you. A price can keep climbing. If you short at 50 dollars and it goes to 100, you have lost 50 dollars a share. If it goes to 200, you have lost 150. In theory, there is no limit to how much a short trade can lose.

There is also a trap called a short squeeze. Picture a crowd of traders who are all short the same thing. The price starts rising. To get out, each of them has to buy. But all that buying pushes the price even higher, which forces more of them to buy. The exit door gets very crowded, very fast.

How I keep a short trade under control

I treat a short exactly like any other trade. The direction changes. The rules do not.

  • I know my exit before I enter. Every short has a stop loss sitting above my entry. That stop is what turns a loss with no ceiling into a small, known amount.
  • I risk the same small slice every time. A short does not get a bigger bet because I feel sure the price is too high.
  • I do not short something just because it went up a lot. Expensive things can get more expensive for a long time. I wait for the price to actually turn before I act.
  • I take the loss when I am wrong. Hoping a short will come back is how a small loss grows into a big one.

Should a beginner try short selling?

You do not have to. Plenty of traders only ever buy. But markets fall as well as rise, and understanding both directions makes you a more complete trader. If you are curious, practice short trades on a practice account first, with no real money on the line. Get used to the feeling of wanting the price to go down.

I will be honest with you. Being able to trade both directions does not make trading easy. Most traders lose money, and short selling does not change that. It just gives you one more way to be right or wrong. What decides how long you last is the same boring thing as always. Keep your risk small and follow your plan. That is the heart of what it takes to trade for a living.

The bottom line

Short selling is selling first and buying back later, hoping the price falls in between. The danger is that a price can rise without limit, so a stop loss and small size are not optional. Treat a short like every other trade you take.

Common questions

What is short selling in simple terms?

Short selling is a bet that a price will fall. You sell first and buy back later, and if the price dropped in between, the difference is your gain.

Can you lose more than you put in when short selling?

Yes. A price has no upper limit, so a short trade with no stop loss can lose more than the amount you started with. That is why a stop loss and small trade size matter so much.

What is a short squeeze?

A short squeeze happens when a rising price forces many short sellers to buy at the same time to get out. Their buying pushes the price even higher, which forces more of them out.

Do you have to borrow anything to go short in futures?

No. In futures you simply take the sell side of a contract, so there are no shares to borrow. With stocks, you borrow the shares from your broker first.

Is short selling good for beginners?

It is not required, and plenty of traders only buy. If you want to learn it, start on a practice account and use the same small risk and stop loss rules as any other trade.
